EDA 101

From specification to tapeout: design stages, artifacts, open-source and commercial software, and real interfaces and demos.

Working on AI4Chip starts with understanding what a model changes, how tools check that change, and which later stages it affects. This page connects the digital ASIC flow to its software and artifacts, with a separate starting point for analog circuits.

From Specification to Tapeout

View full size

The diagram places common tools along the flow. OpenROAD handles physical implementation; OpenROAD Flow Scripts and OpenLane connect multiple tools into runnable flows. Commercial tools such as Genus, Innovus, and Fusion Compiler also work across stages. Projects choose tool combinations around their process, IP, and team setup. Find their entry points in Open Source and Closed Source.

StageMain inputs → artifactsWhat to look for
Specification / Architecture / MicroarchitectureWorkloads, interfaces, and performance targets → module boundaries, pipelines, memory hierarchy, and timing contractsWhether the design meets its targets; how throughput, latency, bandwidth, and resource budgets are allocated
RTL & VerificationSpecification → RTL, testbenches, assertions, waveforms, and test reportsCorrect behavior; coverage of reset, corner cases, and protocols; the cycle where a failure begins
SynthesisRTL, standard-cell libraries, and SDC constraints → gate-level netlist and initial PPA reportsCell mapping, area and timing under the constraints, and preservation of functionality
FloorplanNetlist, macros, and process information → core dimensions, macro/pin locations, and power planningSpace, connectivity, and likely congestion
Placement → CTS (Clock Tree Synthesis) → RoutingFloorplan → cell locations → clock tree → metal connectionsCell placement, clock distribution, and the effects of wiring on timing, congestion, and power
Signoff → TapeoutRouted layout, extracted parasitics, and constraints → check reports, final GDSII / OASIS, and delivery filesWhether STA, power/power integrity, DRC, LVS, and other checks meet project and process requirements

Verification runs throughout the flow. RTL simulation, formal verification, and netlist equivalence checks serve different purposes; STA (Static Timing Analysis) already informs optimization during synthesis. DRC (Design Rule Checking) checks layout geometry against process rules. LVS (Layout Versus Schematic) checks whether the extracted circuit matches the reference netlist. Both belong to physical signoff. Late timing, congestion, or power problems can also require changes to RTL or architecture.

An actual SoC also involves IP integration, DFT, low-power design, and other work. The flow diagrams below show an open-source toolchain, an industrial design flow, and an end-to-end benchmark.

Software, PDKs, and Design Files

EDA software performs simulation, synthesis, or place and route. A PDK supplies process models, layer definitions, and rules; standard-cell libraries supply the available cells and their timing, power, and physical information. The design also needs constraints such as clocks and I/O delays. Changing the process, libraries, or constraints can change the PPA of the same RTL.

On a first run, locate these files: .v / .sv for the design or testbench, .sdc for timing constraints, .lib for cell characteristics, LEF / DEF for physical abstracts and design layout, SPEF for extracted parasitics, and GDSII / OASIS for layout geometry. ORFS also saves reloadable design databases as ODB files. The official ORFS tutorial connects these inputs, directories, and intermediate results.

From Scripts to the GUI

Engineers use the GUI to understand designs and diagnose problems, while scripts make runs repeatable. A typical sequence is:

  1. Load the design, libraries, and constraints through configuration and Tcl scripts, then run a stage.
  2. Read logs and reports to identify failures or metrics that need improvement.
  3. Open the saved database in the GUI and inspect the relevant path, cells, wires, or markers.
  4. Save changes to RTL, constraints, or scripts, rerun, and compare results.

The Berkeley Innovus tutorial shows how scripts and the GUI work together. For a first open-source run, follow the ORFS tutorial from RTL to GDS, then open the synthesis, floorplan, CTS, and routing results separately. Learn to associate each report with a design stage before tuning parameters.

A Separate Path for Analog / Mixed-Signal

Analog design typically starts with topology, device sizes, and performance targets. Its main working objects are schematics and transistor-level circuits:

Specification
    ↓
Schematic & Sizing ↔ SPICE Simulation
    ↓
Layout ↔ DRC / LVS
    ↓
Parasitic Extraction → Post-layout Simulation → Signoff

Start with schematic entry in Xschem and simulation in ngspice: inspect operating points, gain, bandwidth, and transient response before moving to layout, parasitic extraction, and post-layout simulation. Common commercial environments include Cadence Virtuoso / Spectre and Synopsys Custom Compiler / PrimeSim. Begin with chapters 1 and 2 of JKU Analog Circuit Design, which include schematics, simulation examples, and environment setup. Later chapters go deeper into sizing and PVT analysis.
